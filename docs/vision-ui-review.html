The one thing that explains the gap

Structural · read this first

Your app doesn't feel like a robot because, architecturally, it isn't one. It is a web page that borrows your laptop's camera.

Look at what actually runs: frontend/script.js grabs a webcam frame in the browser, JPEG-encodes it, base64s it, and POSTs it to /vision/analyze roughly every 750 ms. The backend decodes it and runs a Haar cascade. Meanwhile edge/camera.py — the module that would actually open the Pi's camera — is never called by the running application. It's dead code.

So the Pi is not seeing anything. The browser is. And every frame takes a round trip through JPEG encode, base64 (which inflates it by a third), HTTP, decode, and back — to achieve about 1.3 frames per second of blurry awareness.

That is why it feels like a chatbot with a webcam bolted on, rather than a robot that is looking at you. The camera has to live in the Pi process, and the browser should only ever receive a video stream and a list of coordinates to draw. Everything else in this document depends on making that one change.

What replaces it

One capture thread in the Pi process owns the camera and writes into a ring buffer. Vision consumers read from that buffer. The UI gets an MJPEG stream for pixels and a WebSocket for JSON boxes, and draws the boxes as positioned <div> elements on top of the video. No frame ever gets encoded twice, and drawing overlays costs the Pi nothing at all.

Where to stand the robot

Camera · real numbers for your 8MP module

Your BOM camera is the 8MP module (IMX219), which has a 62.2° horizontal field of view. That single number answers your question. The width the camera sees is almost exactly 1.2× the distance:

1.0 m1.21 m1–2 people169 pxExcellent

2.5 m3.02 m4–5 people68 pxUnreliable at 720p
3.0 m3.62 m5–6 people57 pxDetection only, no names

So: 1.5 to 2 metres, and capture at 1280×720. You are currently at 0.6 m, which is why your face fills the screen. Nothing is wrong with the camera — you are simply three times too close. Step back to about five feet and it will look right.

The floor for face recognition is roughly 80 pixels of face width, because the embedding models want a 112×112 crop. Detection alone survives down to about 40 px. That threshold is what caps you at 2 m rather than 3 m.

On zoom

The module has no optical zoom, so there is no lens to adjust. But the sensor is 3280×2464 and you are only reading 1280×720 out of it, which means digital zoom is genuinely free — you crop a rectangle of the sensor and still have real pixels, not interpolation. In picamera2 that is the ScalerCrop control.

So expose camera.zoom in config as a 1.0–2.5 multiplier that maps to a centred sensor rectangle. Useful later for pushing in on whoever is speaking. It will not help your current problem though — zooming in at 0.6 m makes it worse. Distance first.

Two things about mounting

  • Put the camera at head height, not on the chest. Your robot is five feet. A chest-mounted camera at ~1.2 m looks at a standing adult's stomach and a seated child's forehead. Head height with a slight downward tilt sees faces at both heights.
  • Consider a wide lens only after recognition works. A 102–120° module covers a whole class from 1.5 m, but it shrinks every face below the recognition floor and adds barrel distortion. Standard 62° for Phase 1 is the right call.

The Bluetooth trap

Audio · worth knowing before you debug it for a week

This will bite you

Bluetooth earphones cannot do good playback and microphone capture at the same time. The moment the mic opens, the profile drops from A2DP (stereo, full bandwidth) to HFP — 8 or 16 kHz, mono, both directions, plus 100–300 ms of extra latency. Your speech recognition accuracy will fall off a cliff and the robot's voice will sound like a phone call from 2004.

If your wake word starts missing or transcription gets worse when the robot is speaking, this is why — not your code.

Fine for today's testing. But your BOM already has the right answer: the ₹250 USB desktop microphone for input, and a separate speaker for output. Never route both directions through one Bluetooth device. Wire the speaker if you can.

Also plan for barge-in now

The mic will hear the robot's own voice and try to transcribe it, or trigger its own wake word. Proper acoustic echo cancellation is real work. The cheap fix that works: mute the microphone while TTS is playing, half-duplex, with a short tail delay. Put it in config as audio.half_duplex: true and revisit only if teachers complain they cannot interrupt.

Wake word

Two sensible options, both config-selectable: openWakeWord (open source, ONNX, no API key, about 10% of one core) or Porcupine (free tier needs a key, lighter and more accurate, easy custom phrases). I'd default to openWakeWord so the robot has no key dependency, and keep Porcupine available for when you want "Hey Lomas" trained properly.

Knowing who is who, cheaply

Recognition · the one design decision that makes this run on a Pi 4

The instinct is to run face recognition on every frame. That will melt a Pi 4 — embedding a face costs 30–80 ms, so five students at 15 fps is roughly six cores of work you do not have.

Recognise once, then track. Identity does not change between frames, so you only pay for recognition when a new face appears:

Recognition ends up running one or two times per student per minute instead of fifteen times a second. That is the whole trick.

For the model, skip face_recognition/dlib — it is 1–2 seconds per face on a Pi 4. Use a MobileFaceNet or ArcFace-lite ONNX model through onnxruntime, or InsightFace's small bundle. Both land in the 30–80 ms range.

Your enrolment video is a good idea

Three to four seconds of the student turning their head left and right is exactly right, and better than a single photo. Sample about 10–15 frames across the sweep, embed each, discard the blurry and extreme-angle ones, and store the average embedding plus the two or three best variants. Multiple angles is what makes recognition survive a child who is looking at their friend rather than the robot.

Store the vectors. Do not store the video or the frames once embedding is done — see the next-but-one section.

Attention, and how not to build a snitch

The nudge feature · technically easy, socially delicate

Technically this is simple once tracking works. Head pose gives you yaw and pitch per student. Define an attention cone in config, score each student as a moving average over ~10 seconds, and when someone sits below threshold for longer than a configured duration, emit an event. The agent decides whether and how to speak.

Every number is config: cone angle, window length, threshold, minimum duration, per-student cooldown, maximum nudges per session. You will tune all of them against real children and none of it should require a code change.

The part I'd think hard about

A robot that announces a child is not paying attention will be hated — by the child, and faster than that, by the teacher. Classroom management is the teacher's authority, and a device that undermines it in front of thirty students will be switched off and never switched on again. This is a product-adoption risk, not just an ethics one.

What works instead: phrase it as curiosity, never correction. “Rahul, what do you think happens next?” is an invitation. “Rahul, you seem distracted” is a reprimand from a machine. Same trigger, completely different product.

Three rules I'd bake in from the start: never rank students by attention on any visible screen; never let a low attention score persist into a report a parent sees; and give the teacher a switch that turns nudging off entirely for a session. Make the last one prominent — teachers trusting they can silence it is what makes them willing to leave it on.

Children's biometric data

Compliance · flagging early because it shapes the design

You are about to store face biometrics of minors and monitor their behaviour, in Indian schools, as a commercial product. I'm not a lawyer and you should get a real one before your first paid pilot, but the specific provision to look at is Section 9 of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. Two things in it matter to you:

  • Processing a child's personal data requires verifiable parental consent.
  • It restricts tracking and behavioural monitoring of children — which is a fair description of an attention-scoring feature.

The Act allows certain classes of data fiduciary to be exempted by notification, and education may well be treated favourably. But “we assumed it would be fine” is not a position you want to be in when a school's legal team asks, or when a competitor raises it during a tender.

Design choices that make this much easier

  • Store embeddings, never images. A face vector cannot be reconstructed into a recognisable photo. Delete enrolment frames the moment the vector is computed.
  • Keep it on the robot. Local SQLite, no cloud sync of biometrics. This also happens to be what offline government schools need anyway.
  • Consent as a first-class record. Enrolment should not complete without a consent reference against that student. Make the software refuse, rather than relying on the school to remember.
  • One-tap deletion per student, and automatic purge at end of term.
  • Attention data is ephemeral. Aggregate it for the session, then discard the per-second detail. You lose nothing pedagogically and shed most of the risk.
  • Run the robot nameless when asked. A config flag that disables recognition entirely, so a school that will not consent still gets a working teaching assistant.

This is also why I'd delete vision_analyzer.py rather than leave the stubs. Apparent-gender and age estimation on children is a headline waiting to happen, it adds nothing to teaching, and having the function names sitting in your repo is enough for a diligence process to ask about it.

The 7-inch screen will not work for a class

Something to decide before the UI is built

A 7-inch display at 1024×600 is about 15 cm wide. At your 1.5–2 m teaching distance, readable body text needs to be roughly 28–32 px — which leaves room for perhaps eight or nine words on a line. For a child in the third row of a class of thirty, it is simply invisible.

So the 7-inch screen cannot be where the lesson content lives. It has exactly one good job: be the robot's face. Expression, name of who it is talking to, current state. That is legible at distance because it is large shapes, not text.

The convenient part

The Pi 4 has two HDMI outputs. Face on the 7-inch chest screen, lesson content on a classroom TV or projector through the second port — two different pages, same event stream, no extra hardware. If a school has no projector, the robot degrades to a one-to-few tutor at 1 m, which is still a real product.

Worth confirming with your first pilot schools whether a display or projector exists in the room. It changes the pitch as much as the code.

The screens

Five robot states, three admin views, one debug overlay

You asked how many screens. Fewer than you'd think — the robot face has five states, and most of what looks like separate screens is the same layout with different content.

Robot face — 7-inch chest display

STATE 01
Sleeping
Ambient. Eyes blink slowly, occasional look-around. A quiet line: “Say Hey Lomas to begin.” This is what a school sees between periods, so it should look alive, not like a screensaver.
STATE 02
Listening
Wake word fired. Eyes widen and orient toward the speaker. Live partial transcript in large type so the student can see they are being heard. The single most reassuring screen in the whole product.
STATE 03
Thinking
Between question and answer. Needs to exist, or a two-second pause reads as a crash. A breathing animation and a glance upward do the whole job.
STATE 04
Speaking
Mouth animates. Current sentence in very large text. Name badge of who is being addressed. This is where the robot spends most of the lesson.
STATE 05
Asking
Quiz or a question back to the room. Options as big numbered cards. Whose turn it is, shown by name.

Across all five, a persistent student ribbon along the bottom: name plus one coloured dot. That is your “showing their name on the UI with boxes” requirement, and it belongs in the frame permanently rather than as its own screen.

Teacher and admin — laptop or tablet, over WiFi

ADMIN A
Enrolment
Live camera, name and class fields, consent checkbox, record 3–4 seconds, quality feedback while the head turns, then confirm. Shows the embedding count, never the stored images.
ADMIN B
Live control
Camera with boxes, the roster with attention state, pause and resume, skip section, tap-to-attribute who is speaking, and the nudging on/off switch. The teacher's steering wheel.
ADMIN C
Session report
After class: who attended, what was covered, questions asked, quiz results per student, trend across sessions. This is the artefact that justifies the subscription.

Debug — one overlay, not a screen

DEBUG
Diagnostics overlay
Your existing dashboard, essentially: FPS, detection count, recognition latency, CPU and temperature, audio levels, wake-word confidence, last serial frames, full LLM prompt and token count. Toggled by config, drawn over any view.

That is nine surfaces total, and only five of them are the robot. Your current app has roughly nine cards on one screen — the work is mostly redistribution, not new invention.

Face left, one sentence right, students along the bottom. Green dot engaged, amber drifting, grey not in frame. No numbers, no percentages, no charts — nothing a child could read as a score about themselves.

The principles behind that

  • One idea on screen at a time. The current sentence, nothing else. Children read slowly and the robot is speaking anyway — text is reinforcement, not the channel.
  • Type large enough to be wrong about. Start at 32 px body on the 7-inch and go up. If it looks absurdly big on your laptop over VNC, it is probably right on the actual panel at 1.5 m.
  • The face is not decoration. It is the single cheapest thing you can build that makes children treat the machine as a participant. Eyes that track toward whoever is speaking are worth more than any other feature on this list, and cost almost nothing.
  • Attention shown as colour, never as number. A dot changes gently. A percentage invites comparison and makes a child feel measured.
  • Three colours, not nine. Your current UI has badges in every hue. Pick a ground, one accent, and semantic green/amber — and let size carry hierarchy instead of colour.
  • Animate the face, never the layout. Blink, breathe, glance, mouth movement. But do not slide panels around — on a Pi driving Chromium, layout animation is where your frame rate goes to die.
A cheap trick worth doing

Drive the mouth from TTS audio amplitude rather than trying to sync phonemes. Sample the output buffer, map loudness to mouth height, done in about twenty lines. Nobody can tell the difference, and it reads as genuinely alive.

Making the camera module reusable

Pluggable code · your specific request

You want a camera file you can drop into any project. That works if you enforce one rule: a capability package may import its own dependencies and nothing else of yours. No imports from siblings, no imports from the app, no shared config object passed in.

Concretely, lomas_vision becomes an installable package with its own config schema, its own registry, and a factory:

  • A CameraSource protocol — open, read, close, set_zoom. That is the whole contract.
  • Implementations, one file each: picamera2, usb (OpenCV), file (a video, for testing without hardware), folder (still images, for deterministic tests), mock.
  • Callers do camera = create_camera(cfg) and never learn which one they got.

The payoff is bigger than reuse. With a file backend, your whole vision pipeline becomes testable on a laptop with no Pi and no camera — you record one classroom video and replay it, and every recognition and attention change is reproducible. That single backend will save your interns more time than any other decision in this document.

Same pattern for lomas_speech, lomas_llm, lomas_face. Four small packages, each independently installable, each usable in your next product without dragging LomasAI along. The application becomes thin glue over them.

The rule that keeps it true

Add a test that walks each package's imports and fails if any of them reference a sibling package or the app. Without it, someone will import a config helper “just this once” in month two and the package stops being portable without anyone noticing.

Two conclusions from that table. First, the browser costs more than all the vision work combined, so keep the page lean — no React, no chart libraries, CSS transitions only, and never redraw a canvas every frame. Second, keep the LLM in the cloud. A Pi 4 cannot run a useful model at conversational latency, and Groq's speed is genuinely the right pick for this; your existing router already does the sensible thing.

Add active cooling. Your BOM has the 4010 fans — sustained vision work will throttle a bare Pi 4 within about fifteen minutes, and a throttled Pi looks exactly like a software bug.

What I'd build, in order

Sequence · each step visible on the screen before the next starts

01
Invert the camera
picamera2 capture in the Pi process, ring buffer, MJPEG stream to the browser, boxes as JSON over WebSocket drawn as HTML overlay. Nothing new functionally — but it is the foundation everything else sits on, and it will feel dramatically more responsive immediately.
02
Config and the four packages
Layered YAML with a typed schema, and the split into lomas_vision / lomas_speech / lomas_llm / lomas_face with the file-backend camera. Do this before the features, not after — retrofitting is where projects like this go to die.
03
Enrolment and names on screen
The admin enrolment screen, the 3-second sweep, embeddings in SQLite, then detect-track-identify running live with names in boxes. The first genuinely new capability, and the one that will convince you the product is real.
04
Wake word and the face
openWakeWord, half-duplex audio, and the five face states with animation. At the end of this step you can say “Hey Lomas”, be greeted by name, and hold a conversation with something that looks alive.
05
Teaching flow and attention
Topic selection, lesson segments, the Q&A loop, then attention scoring and gentle nudges. This is where the session flow from the earlier plan finally connects to the vision work.
06
Teacher control and reports
Live control screen and the session report. The part that turns a demo into something a school will pay for annually.

Steps 1 and 2 are unglamorous and I would still not skip them. Everything after gets roughly twice as fast because of them.

Back to you

What I need before building

  1. Face content, projector content — or accept small groups? The 7-inch screen decision shapes the entire UI. Do your target schools have a TV or projector in the room?
  2. Is nameless mode a requirement? If some schools will refuse face recognition, the teaching flow has to work without identity from day one, not as a later patch.
  3. How many students in frame for Phase 1? Three or four at 1.5–2 m is comfortable and recognisable. Thirty is a different camera and a different product. I'd start small deliberately.
  4. Keep Groq and DeepSeek? Your router and clients already work. I'd keep them behind the provider interface rather than rewrite, unless you want something else as default.
  5. Who owns consent? The enrolment screen needs to record something. School-level blanket consent, or per-student? Affects the schema.

Answer those and I'll start on step 1.
